## 3. Difficult technical decision

**Situation:** Choose continue investing in an expensive observability platform vs build a custom analytics solution.

**Task:** Decide across functionality, reliability, ops complexity, security, migration risk, effort, and long-term cost.

**Action:** Phased migration — identify must-keep capabilities → build replacement → validate on production workloads → run in parallel → migrate incrementally.

**Result:** **$1M+/yr** savings while preserving observability and security.

**Close:** Architecture decisions are business value + risk, not technology preference.

## 4. Conflict with another senior engineer / architect

**Situation:** Disagreement on architecture for a high-volume transaction component — distributed approach vs keeping more in the existing service.

**Action:** Defined objective criteria (scalability, latency, ops complexity, failure handling, effort, extensibility). Reviewed with architecture group. Adopted a **hybrid** using the strongest parts of both.

**Result:** Decision by measurable criteria, not seniority or ego.

**Close:** Strong technical leadership includes changing your mind when evidence wins.

## 5. Disagreed with your manager

**Situation:** Proposed timeline created material operational/technical risk.

**Action:** Instead of “can’t be done,” presented three options:
1. Full scope on original timeline (higher risk)
2. Reduce scope — critical capabilities first
3. Extend timeline for complete lower-risk delivery

**Result:** Leadership chose phased delivery; highest-priority capabilities shipped with lower overall risk.

**Close:** Convert “engineering resistance” into a **business trade-off** discussion.

## 11. Decision with incomplete information

**Action:** Separated **reversible** vs **irreversible** decisions. Moved on reversible with assumptions; for irreversible, identified data that could change the call and added validation gates. Added monitoring and success criteria.

**Close:** Move fast on reversible decisions; be deliberate on irreversible ones.

---

## 16. Challenged the status quo

**Situation:** Platform “worked” but cost and scale trajectory were worsening; default was to extend it.

**Action:** Analyzed long-term cost, scalability, ops complexity, and engineering effort. Proposed **phased modernization**, not big-bang replacement.

**Result:** Significant cost savings and more scalable architecture.

**Close:** Ask: *Is the current solution still best for the business we have today?*

---

## 18. Changed your mind

**Situation:** Initially favored a more scalable-looking design.

**Action:** Peer presented benchmarks showing a simpler design met real requirements with far less ops complexity. Reviewed data; changed recommendation; **explicitly explained why** so the team saw evidence-driven leadership.

**Result:** Simpler approach adopted.

**Close:** Being “right” matters less than getting the organization to the right answer.

---

## 22. Had to say “No”

**Situation:** Feature would add large architectural complexity for small business value.

**Action:** Explained long-term ops/maintenance cost; proposed a simpler alternative that captured most value.

**Close:** Don’t just say no — explain the trade-off and offer an alternative.
